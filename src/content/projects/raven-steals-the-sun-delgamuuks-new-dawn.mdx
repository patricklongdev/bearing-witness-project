Carlos Mendez In his bold and vibrant painting, Raven Steals the Sun/*Delgamuukw’s* New Dawn, for  the 2010 Aboriginal and Treaty Rights Seminar drew upon the “Raven Travelling” oral histories common to many Northwest Coast Indigenous peoples and how the artistic licence of colour helped give a deeper meaning to his reflection upon the tragedy of colonial history and the more hopeful narratives in the evolving jurisprudence on Aboriginal Rights and Title.

In his accompanying essay Carlos explains how he “increasingly found myself locating analogues to the tale of raven in the many historical narratives recounted in class” and how “thinking and feeling this issue through the medium of art has endowed a new and deeper layer of meaning to what I have long held to be the fundamental tragedy at the heart of Canadian history”.

>Raven Steals the Sun 
>
>While many variations of 'raven stealing the sun' abound, most follow a similar trajectory. 
>
>Near the beginning of time, the world was engulfed in darkness. The sun, that giver of life and hope, was being guarded by a powerful chief. The chief had been asked by the creator to protect the sun and share it with the people and creatures of the world. However, much to the creator's dismay, the chief kept the sun greedily hidden in a box, and as a consequence, all was dark. Seeing the injustice and despair caused by the chief's greed, raven decided to find a way to give the light back to the world. One day, as the chief's beloved daughter sat under the blackened skies drinking from her favourite cup, raven transformed himself into a cedar leaf and, under the cover of darkness, fell into the vessel. Raven was swallowed by the chief's daughter, and once inside of her, he transformed himself into a young baby. When the chief's daughter gave birth to a mysterious child, many in the village harboured suspicion, but being his kin and the child of his beloved daughter, the chief embraced the baby. Much to the chief's surprise, the young child soon became enamoured with the box that contained the light. While at first the chief denied the child's requests, after persistent efforts, the chief acquiesced, carefully handing him the box. However, as soon as the box was open, raven transformed back to his true form and, seizing the sun in his beak, flew up to the sky to give the light back to the people and creatures of the world.
>
>As our term together progressed, I increasingly found myself locating analogues to the tale of raven in the many historical narratives recounted in class. Through colonial conquest, desecration by disease, treaties whose written meanings greatly diverged from those represented to elders, and Canada's own trail of tears - the horrors of residential schooling, the darkness of our collective history as Canadians can be overwhelming. The darkness was, in effect, complete when the Canadian govemment, a body responsible for the welfare of its peoples, made it illegal for any lawyer to take on an Aboriginal rights case.
>
>However, with the lifting of this affront to justice and human rights, a handful of dedicated men and women, both inside and outside of the legal community, forged a new and brighter dawn for Canada's first peoples. It is a narrative that stretches through Calder and the activism of a new generation of Aboriginal leadership in the 1970's, to the inclusivity and successes of the Berger Commission, section 35(1), *Guerin, Sparrow, Van der Peet and, of course, Delgamuukw*. 
>
>Like the raven, the visionaries who fought for justice gained the confidence of those who tried to keep the light hidden from view, and in doing so leamed how the system could be changed from within. Guided by a firm belief that law can be a powerful agent of social change, legal challenges were mounted, and in a series of decisions culminating in *Delgamuukw the light was given back to the people. 
>
>From the monumental recognition of Aboriginal title in BC by the Supreme Court of Canada after so many years of denial by provincial governments, to an acknowledgement that law needs to be more inclusive of aboriginal perspectives and historical transmission, and the establishment of a duty to consult and accommodate, *Delgamuukw* laid the foundation fora promising new chapter in Canadian history. 
>
>The image on the canvas thus represents the light of justice being given to the Aboriginal peoples of British Columbia following Delgamuukw. With the text of the decision inscribed upon the sun, my hope is that for the viewer familiar with both the story of raven stealing the sun and the *Delgamuukw* decision the meaning of the image should be relatively clear. 
>
>The backdrop of blue and black in the image represents the darkness of a world without light. The blue, brown and green patches of colour represent the water, earth and plants of the land to which BC's Aboriginal peoples were denied title. Finally, while the yellow patch of colour represents the light of hope that never died amongst those committed to social justice, the prominent use of red, both as a free floating patch of colour and as a grounding foundation below, represents the tragic histories of loss and suffering that have bedevilled Canada's Aboriginal peoples. 
>
>While the execution of the final product may have, at times, caused me frustration, the weaving of history, law and art has had a powerful effect on me both as a soon-to-be lawyer and as a Canadian. 
>
>Thinking and feeling this issue through the medium of art has endowed a new and deeper layer of meaning to what I have long held to be the fundamental tragedy at the heart of Canadian history. In the final analysis, through the manner in which the light was given back to the people, the nature of this tragedy as a challenge that all Canadians must face has been made clear to me. I appreciate the opportunity this project has given me to see our collective history in these terms.
