Grant Sikkes for his project for the Penal Policy seminar in 2014 chose as the template for his paper mosaic the photo taken by my son, ShaneJackson, that was used for the cover of *Justice behind the Walls: Human Rights in Canadian Prisons*, Titled "Pieces of a Man" Grant examines the impact of administrative segregation.

![Grant Sikkes's 2014 mosaic project titled "Pieces of a Man"](/assets/2014/02/Grant_Sikkes_02.jpg)

![Shane Jackson's 1999 photo from the cover of "Justice Behind the Walls"](/assets/2014/02/Grant_Sikkes_03.jpg)

In his accompanying essay Grant explains why he chose mosaic as his medium.

>I chose to create a mosaic that I have titled 'Pieces of a Man' as my final project for Michael Jackson's seminar on penal policy. The image that I have created is a representation of one of Shane Jackson's photographs from the book, *Justice Behind the Walls: Human Rights in Canadian Prisons*. It depicts an inmate, Jeff Blackburn, at the Matsqui Institution looking out of his segregation cell from the food slot. I find the image to by extraordinarily powerful because of the nature of administrative segregation within the prison system and because the photograph viscerally captures Mr. Blackburn's isolation. I wanted to recreate the photograph as a mosaic because I feel the medium demonstrates the effect of incarceration, and particularly administrative segregation, on a person's humanity. 
>
>Imprisonment, literally and figuratively, breaks down the individual. It fractures one's identity, and renders him as a "broken" figure or one that is "in need of fixing." Edgar Roussel in an interview with Michael Jackson expressed that idea as follows, "[t]he system aims to reduce the criminal to nothing, restrain the slightest initiative, and in one word, assassinate his personality to make him conform...All of the small parts of the mosaic come together to form the larger picture. When Jeff Blackburn stares out from his food slot, it is the culmination of countless discrete processes that have lead to that particular moment. Some of the processes are within his control and many of them are not. This is especially so in administrative segregation where there is an abject lack of control and autonomy over his liberty.
>
>Administrative segregation is the extreme end of imprisonment and is used by the CSC as a corrective measure to control inmate behavior. Humans are by nature social creatures and to deny a person virtually all contact with others is in many ways a denial of their humanity. As Michael Jackson notes, "Where else in the prison system can a prisoner who is socially isolated feel even more isolated? Where else can a prisoner with cognition problems, poor conflict resolution skills, and a low frustration tolerance experience conditions in which these are put to the most severe test on a daily basis?" This is best expressed by Gary Weaver when he said, "slowly but surely, you lose your sense of self and your sense of community." 

>Fyodor Dostoyevsky once said that, "you can judge the civilization of a society by entering its prisons." I think that this rings as true today as when he wrote it. How the prisons operate in Canada, as well as the effect that prison law and administration has on its prisoners operates as a benchmark for the rest of society to judge itself. It's a scary proposition to think that anyone's right to humanity and dignity can be transgressed so easily by the state. Once it occurs, you become the fractured, the broken, and the assassinated. I cannot begin to understand the isolation and brutality that Jeff Blackburn faced during his 74 days in segregation. The image will remain with me throughout the rest of my legal career, I tried to honor it by recreating it and to remember that inside those walls were the 'Pieces of a Man'.

Grant’s mosaic has a special significance for my own work in challenging the practice of administrative segregation. The essay quotes from Edgar Roussel and Gary Weaver, two of the prisoners who I interviewed for my books and who have the  rare gift of being able to conjure the language to express what for those of us who have never experience it is inexpressible. In *Prisoners of Isolation* I invoked a quote from Herman Heses in *Magister Ludi* to explain the difficulty prisoners have in describing the experience of solitary confinement:

>To express in words something that refuses to be put into words …what gives these experiences their weight and persuasiveness is not their truth …but their reality. They are tremendously real, somewhat the way a violent physical pain or a surprising natural event, a storm or earthquake, seem to us charged with an entirely different sort of reality, presence, inexorability, from ordinary times and conditions.

The fragmentation of Grant’s mosaic conveys visually what I experienced when I interviewed Edgar Roussel in 1982 in the first generation of the Special Handling Units, Canada’s Super Maximum Prisons. In Prisoners of Isolation I described That Interview:

>I spent two hours with Edgar Roussel in the interview room of block 5. We were separated by a wall on top of which was a thick lattice-wire grill. Looking through that grill it was impossible to see both of Edgar Roussel’s eyes at once. After about thirty minutes his face no longer appeared as a whole face but rather as disconnected fragments. Before my eyes, Edgar Roussel appeared to disintegrate. The statement in his letter to the Honourable Mark McGuigan that the aim of the SHU is to ‘assassinate’ the prisoner’s personality never seemed more real.

Thirty years later Shane’s photograph of Jeff Blackburn staring out through the food slot of his segregation so let Matsqui institution frames the inhuman caging of segregation. Together with Grant’s composition of his fragmented mosaic, along with other projects in the Archive, they visually and graphically honour the education that prisoners, through their lived experiences, have provided me, my students and the legal community about the dehumanizing effects of administrative segregation. They illustrate that the Life of the Law as Art helps to widen our understanding of the justice /injustice of imprisonment.

A selection of Shane’s photographs of the people and spaces of Matsqui and Kent Institutions that were used in Illustrating *Justice behind the Walls* are the subject of a 2002 Maclean’s magazine feature photo essay which can be [viewed here](https://bearing-witness-project.patrickdavidlong.workers.dev/assets/2026/06/Shane_Jackson_Macleans_Photo_Essay.pdf).
